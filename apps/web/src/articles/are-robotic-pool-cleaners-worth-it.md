## The short answer

For a pool you own and swim in through a full season, yes.

A robotic pool cleaner takes about an hour a week of brushing and vacuuming and
turns it into something you drop in the water and forget. Unlike a suction
cleaner it carries its own motor and its own filter, so the debris comes out of
circulation instead of being pushed into your pump basket. That is the whole
value proposition and it is a real one.

It is not worth it in four specific situations, and those are worth reading
first, because a robot bought into one of them is an expensive machine that sits
in a garage.

## The four cases where it is not worth it

**A small above-ground pool you drain each winter.** The machine costs a
meaningful fraction of the pool. Most robots are not rated for a vinyl liner at
all, and the ones that are tend to be floor-only. If the floor is genuinely the
job, an above-ground rated machine like the Aiper Seagull SE is the honest
purchase — but a net and twenty minutes is a legitimate answer here in a way it
is not for a 40 ft in-ground pool.

**A pool already cleaned by a service you are keeping.** You are buying a machine
to do work you are already paying somebody to do. Robots do not reduce a service
visit; they replace it or they duplicate it.

**A pool whose actual problem is chemistry.** Cloudy water, algae bloom and green
tint are filtration and sanitiser problems. A robot vacuums; it does not balance
water. Two of the machines we cover claim some form of clarification, but if the
pool goes green every August the fix is upstream of any robot.

**A pool that is mostly clean and mostly unused.** The saving is time, and time
is only saved if you were spending it.

## What you are actually buying

An hour a week, and less wear on the plant you already own.

The second part gets less attention than it deserves. A suction cleaner runs off
your pump: everything it picks up travels through the plumbing to the pump basket
and the filter, and your filtration system does the work and wears accordingly. A
robot is a self-contained machine with its own motor and its own filter cartridge.
It runs whether or not the pump is on, and what it collects never reaches your
filter at all.

Anyone selling you a robot on electricity savings is selling you something else.
The numbers are small, they depend on your pump and your rates, and they are not
why anybody keeps one.

## Robot, suction or pressure

Three product classes, and the confusion between them is why "robotic" gets used
loosely.

- **Suction cleaners** attach to your skimmer or a dedicated line and use the
  pump's suction. Cheap, no electronics, and everything goes to your filter.
- **Pressure cleaners** run off the return line, often with a booster pump, and
  bag their own debris. Good on leaves, mediocre on fine silt.
- **Robotic cleaners** are independent: their own motor, their own filter, their
  own navigation. They are the only class that reliably climbs walls, and the
  only one that scrubs the waterline.

If your complaint is the ring of scum at the surface rather than what is on the
floor, the waterline is a robot-only capability — and not every robot has it.

## What it costs to keep

Filters are the running cost and nobody mentions them. Cartridges and fine
filter bags need rinsing after most cycles and replacing periodically, and a
machine whose filter is awkward to reach is one you will clean less often than
you should.

The other number is the warranty, and it is the one worth comparing because
nobody will commit to a lifespan. Terms across our catalogue run from one to
three years, with a single 3-year full replacement term on the AquaSense 2 Ultra.
On a cordless machine the warranty is doing extra work: it is covering a battery,
which is the one part guaranteed to hold less charge each season and is rarely
user-replaceable. That is the case for a corded machine like the Dolphin Nautilus
CC Plus, and the full argument sits on the cordless page.

## If the answer is yes

The next question is which one, and that is a question about your pool rather
than about robots. Length, type, whether the walls actually get dirty, whether
the waterline is the real complaint, whether anything overhangs the water.
