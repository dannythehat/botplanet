## The short answer

Yes, in the middle of the pane. Not at the edges.

That single sentence is the whole honest review of this category, and it is the
one thing no manufacturer will print. A window robot will take a pane that is
hazy with dust and rain marks and leave it genuinely clear across most of its
area. Then it will reach the last centimetre against the frame, and it will not
do that part as well as you would with a cloth.

Whether that is worth it depends entirely on how many windows you have. For four
windows, no — the setup takes longer than the job. For a house with glass
everywhere, or windows you cannot safely reach, it is the difference between a
chore you keep putting off and one that happens.

## How they actually stay on the glass

A fan. That is the surprise, and it explains most of the behaviour.

The machine holds itself up by pulling air out from underneath, which creates
lower pressure inside than outside, and the atmosphere presses it against the
window. It is not a magnet and it is not glue. The practical consequences follow
directly:

- **They are loud.** You cannot make strong suction quietly. Expect something
  closer to a vacuum cleaner than to an appliance you forget about.
- **They need a seal.** A cracked pane, a heavy raised bar, or thick dirt
  breaking contact all reduce the grip.
- **Losing power means losing suction**, which is why the safety systems below
  exist and why you should care about them.

Two pads underneath do the cleaning, wet with a fine spray, and the machine
drives itself over the glass in a pattern while the pads drag. Some models spray
through nozzles, some use an ultrasonic atomiser that produces a much finer mist.
Both work. Neither scrubs the way a hand does.

## What stops it falling

Two things, and you should understand both before you put one above a doorway.

**A battery that only exists for the fall.** Almost every machine here runs on
mains power. The internal battery is not for cleaning — it is there so that when
the power cuts, the fan keeps turning long enough for you to get the machine
down. Makers call this power-off protection, and the number that matters is how
many minutes it holds. Some publish it. Many do not, and where a maker writes a
reassuring phrase and no duration, treat it as a phrase.

**A physical rope.** Every one of these ships with a safety rope, and it is not
optional decoration. Use it on every window above the ground floor, every single
time, anchored to something that is not the window. This is the only part of the
category where the failure mode is a machine falling from a height onto whatever
is below.

## Where they genuinely disappoint

**The edges.** Said again because it is the thing people are surprised by. A
robot travels in a path and turns before the frame, so the border of the pane
gets the least contact and the corners get least of all. A machine specifically
built for edges helps. Nothing eliminates it.

**Streaking, in the wrong conditions.** Direct sun on the glass dries the spray
before the pad reaches it, which is the same reason you are told not to wash a
car in the sun. Clean on an overcast day or out of direct light and most of the
complaints about streaking disappear.

**Really dirty glass.** These are maintenance machines. A window with a season of
grime on it wants a first pass by hand; the robot keeps it clear afterwards.

**One side at a time.** They clean the face they are placed on. The outside of an
upstairs window is only reachable if the window opens far enough inwards for you
to place the machine on the outer face and attach the rope. No machine we hold
does both sides at once, and no magnetic robot that would is currently sold in
the US.

## When the answer is no

Four situations where we would tell you not to buy one.

**You have a handful of windows.** Filling the tank, fitting the pads, setting the
rope and supervising the first run is not less work than cleaning four panes.

**Your glass is frameless and the machine does not say so.** A robot that
navigates by feeling for a frame will drive off the edge of a pane that has none.
Where a maker does not claim frameless glass, that silence is the answer.

**Your real problem is the outside of sealed upstairs windows.** Nothing here
solves it. That is a genuine gap in what is sold, not a gap in our catalogue.

**You want the finish you get by hand.** You will not get it at the edges. If the
last centimetre against the frame is the part that bothers you, this is the wrong
purchase and no amount of specification changes it.

## So are they worth it

For a house with a lot of glass, yes, and the reason is not the finish.

It is that the windows get done at all. A robot turns a job you schedule and
dread into one you start and walk away from, and glass that is cleaned often at
ninety per cent looks considerably better than glass cleaned perfectly twice a
year. That is the actual case for the category, and it is a real one.

For a flat with four windows, buy a squeegee.
