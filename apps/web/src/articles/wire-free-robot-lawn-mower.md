## The short answer

Wire-free is worth paying for when your lawn is going to change, and worth
skipping when it is not.

Search for a wire free robot lawn mower and you get three different navigation
technologies wearing one label. Two of them work beautifully on open ground and
fall over under a mature tree. The third works under the tree and costs more
again. Which one you need is decided by what sits above your grass, not by what
is printed on the box.

If your garden is a simple rectangle you have no plans to redesign, a boundary
wire is cheaper, steadier and completely unglamorous. That is the honest
starting position, and most of the marketing in this category is written to talk
you out of it.

## Three technologies, one label

**RTK satellite.** The mower carries a satellite receiver, and a fixed antenna
sits on the house or on a post in the garden. The pair correct each other, which
is what takes ordinary GPS from metre-level accuracy down to centimetres. This
is what most buyers mean by a gps robot lawn mower, and it is the system sold on
the majority of wire-free machines today.

**Vision.** Cameras read the line between grass and everything else, roughly the
way you do. No antenna, no sky needed. It is also the system most easily
confused by an overgrown edge, a lawn that has gone to seed, or low winter sun
straight into the lens.

**LiDAR.** A spinning laser builds a map of the solid objects around the
machine. A lidar robot lawn mower does not care about tree cover at all, because
it never looks upwards for anything. It is the newest of the three here and the
dearest.

Several machines now combine two of them. That is not specification padding —
vision and satellite cover each other's blind spots, and the pairing is worth
the money on a garden that has both open lawn and a canopy.

## When the wire is still the right answer

A boundary wire is a cable pinned or buried around the edge of the lawn. It is
cheap, it does not lose signal, and once it is down it stays down.

It wins outright in four situations:

- **Mature trees over the grass.** A satellite system needs a clear view of the
  sky, and a canopy takes it away. This is the single most common reason a
  wire-free machine disappoints its owner.
- **A small, settled lawn.** You lay the cable once, in an afternoon, and never
  think about it again.
- **A tight budget.** The wire is by far the cheapest route into a robot lawn
  mower, and a cheap robot lawn mower running on a wire will cut your grass
  perfectly well.
- **A tall building along one edge.** Walls bounce satellite signal and produce
  the same drift a tree does.

The wire has one failure mode worth knowing before you commit: strimmers cut it.
A break is a connector and ten minutes, not a catastrophe, but it happens more
often than any manufacturer's page admits.

## What wire-free actually buys you

Flexibility. That is the whole of it, and it is worth more than it sounds.

Moving a border, adding a bed, or fencing off a vegetable patch is a minute on a
phone rather than an afternoon with a pegging tool. Splitting a garden into
zones — front, back, the strip down the side — stops being a wiring problem and
becomes a line you drag. If you are the sort of person who rearranges a garden,
that is the argument.

What it does not buy you is a better cut. Navigation decides where the machine
goes, not what it does to the grass when it gets there. Both kinds mulch the
same way, take the same few millimetres off, and leave the same edges for you to
strim.

It also does not buy you traction. A machine that cannot hold a slope cannot
hold it with or without a cable, and that is a wheel, weight and blade-deck
question answered on our page for the best robot lawn mower for hills.

## The question people actually ask

What people mean by a robot lawn mower without perimeter wire is usually one
specific worry: will it wander into next door's garden, or into the pond.

The honest answer is that RTK holds a line to a few centimetres when it can see
the sky, and drifts when it cannot. Manufacturers publish the good number and
not the bad one. A machine that loses its fix will normally stop and complain
rather than keep mowing, which is the right behaviour, but it means a garden
with patchy signal produces a mower that halts a lot — and a mower that halts a
lot is one you end up pushing back to its dock by hand.

Judge the claim by where the antenna has to go. If the installation guide wants
it high, clear and away from the house wall, the system is satellite-dependent
and the tree question applies to you.

## What to do next

Stand in your garden and look up.

Clear sky over most of the lawn, and a layout you might change: RTK satellite,
and the extra money is well spent. Heavy tree cover: either LiDAR, a
vision-based machine, or the wire — and the wire will be the cheapest of the
three by a distance. An acre or more of open ground with a shed roof to mount an
antenna on is the best case wire-free has.
