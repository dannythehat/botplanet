## The short answer

Buy the simplest machine that does the one thing you are actually trying to fix,
and buy it for a person rather than for a diagnosis.

A robotic pet for elderly use is a different product from the desk toys and
conversational machines that dominate this category. It has to work with no
phone, no account, no app and no setup. It has to be the right weight in a lap.
And it has to keep working on the day nobody is there to help it, which rules
out most of the interesting machines on the market.

There are three genuinely different products sold to this reader, they answer
three different problems, and buying the wrong one is the usual mistake.

## The three things being sold

**A simple robotic animal.** Battery-powered, fur-covered, purrs or barks,
responds to touch and light. No screen, no internet, nothing to log into. Put
batteries in and hand it over. This is the product with actual research behind
it and the one most likely to be used a year later.

**A social or care robot.** A screen, a voice, a microphone, reminders,
video-calling, sometimes a subscription that carries the service.
An elderly care robot in this sense is a communication device wearing a friendly
face, and it lives or dies on whether the person will talk to a machine at all.

**A monitoring device with a personality.** Sold as company, bought for
reassurance by somebody who lives elsewhere. If checking in is the real
motivation, a pet camera robot or a plain sensor is a more honest purchase, and
usually a cheaper one.

Being clear about which of the three you want takes ten minutes and saves most
of the disappointment in this category.

## Where the evidence actually is

The simple robotic animal is the one with peer-reviewed work behind it.

Studies of robotic pets in dementia care and with socially isolated older adults
report reduced agitation, improved mood and more social interaction on the ward
or in the room. The effect is real and it is modest, and it is measured mostly
in care settings rather than in a house where somebody lives alone.

What that evidence does not say is that the machine replaces company. It says a
person with something to stroke is calmer than a person with nothing, which is a
smaller and more useful claim than the marketing makes.

A robotic pet for dementia is also judged on things nobody lists on a
specification sheet: does it startle, is the noise it makes soothing or
alarming, does it need explaining. A machine that has to be understood before it
can be enjoyed has already failed this reader.

## What to check before buying

**Weight and where it sits.** A lap animal wants to be heavy enough to feel
present and light enough to lift with one hand. A desktop companion robot that
lives on a table is a completely different proposition and suits a different
person — someone who wants something to look at rather than something to hold.

**Batteries, not charging docks.** A dock the person has to remember, aim at and
plug in is a machine that runs flat and stays flat. Replaceable batteries
somebody can change on a visit are more dependable than a rechargeable that
depends on a routine.

**Sound.** Volume control that goes genuinely low, and a switch that turns the
sound off without turning the machine off. Hearing aids and squeaky motors are a
bad pairing.

**No account required.** Anything that needs a phone, a login or a home network
introduces a person who has to maintain it. If that person is you and you live
two hours away, assume it will stop working and nobody will mention it.

**What happens if the company folds.** The talking machines in this category are
services as much as products. A subscription that lapses can take the
personality with it, and the reason our companion robot pages give that risk its
own section is that it has already happened to buyers here more than once.

**Washability.** Food, drink and time all happen. A cover that comes off matters
more than any feature on the box.

## The part most guides skip

How it will be received.

A companion robot for elderly relatives can land as a kindness or as an insult,
and which one it is has almost nothing to do with the machine. Handing somebody
a toy animal implies something about how you see them, and some people find that
unbearable while others take to it immediately.

The two things that seem to make the difference: give it without ceremony, and
give it a name rather than a purpose. "This is for your dementia" is a different
gift entirely from "the cat needs somewhere to live."

If the person has said clearly that they do not want one, they do not want one.
That is the rule-out for this entire page, and it is worth more than any
specification.

## What we cannot tell you yet

Whether the person will find it charming or patronising.

That is the one thing no specification answers and the one that decides whether
a robotic pet gets used or put in a cupboard. Some people take to a pretend
animal immediately. Others find being handed one insulting, and they are not
wrong to. Ask, if you can ask. If you cannot, weigh how the person has reacted
to being helped before.

## What to do next

Answer one question: is the problem loneliness, agitation, or your own worry
from a distance.

Loneliness or agitation, and the person is receptive: a simple robotic animal,
no screen, batteries you can change. Worry from a distance: buy a monitoring
device and stop calling it a companion. Someone sharp, curious and bored: a
robot pet with conversation may suit, but read what the subscription covers
before you pay for it. If the person would rather have a dog than a cat, our
