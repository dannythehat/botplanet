There is no remote and no app. You lay cards on the floor and the mouse
follows them.

Each card is a move — forward, turn left, turn right. A child lines a row of
them up on a grid mat leading to a wedge of plastic cheese, then places the
mouse on the sequence and lets it run. Change the cards, change the path.
That is the entire interface.

## The simplest coding toy in this category

Botley uses a physical remote. Sphero's indi reads colour cards through a
sensor. This is the plainest of the three: cards on a mat and a mouse that
follows whichever sequence is in front of it. Rated for age 4 and up, which
ties Sphero's indi for the youngest floor in the coding-robot lineup, and the
mechanism is simple enough to justify it.

What that trades away is obvious. There is nothing to grow into here — no
app, no block editor, no text mode waiting a year down the line. This is a
toy for finding out whether the idea of "instructions in order" clicks at
all, not a platform.

## Who this is for

Four to seven, and specifically a household that wants the cheapest possible
answer to "does my child like this kind of thing" before spending more.
Against Sphero's Mini at $50 or Botley's activity set in the $60-80 range,
this sits underneath both.

## Who should look elsewhere

**A child already coding with Sphero or Ozobot.** This is not a step down in
quality, it is a step down in age band — a five-to-seven-year-old already
driving a Sphero BOLT in Scratch has outgrown what a card sequence teaches.

**Anyone who wants a growth path in the box.** See above. This is a single
toy, not the entry point to a wider app or coding language.
