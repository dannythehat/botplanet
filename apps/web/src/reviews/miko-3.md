Miko 3 is a two-pound robot on wheels with a screen for a face, sold for $299
and rated by its maker for ages five to ten. Say "Hey Miko" and it answers.

It is the only product in this category designed around a child from the start
rather than adapted for one, and that shows in the things that are easy to
overlook: it drives itself, it recognises a face, it works in eight languages,
and a parent app sits behind all of it.

The question worth answering before any of that is what the $299 actually buys,
because a subscription sits next to it and the marketing does not separate the
two.

## Does Miko 3 need a subscription

No. It works out of the box and keeps working if you never pay Miko another
penny.

That is the honest short answer and it is incomplete, so here is Miko's own
comparison table, read from its product page rather than summarised from
memory.

**Free, in full:** Spell Bee, Dance Master, and the ability to lock apps.

**Limited without Miko Max:** the Learning Buddy, Story Maker, Disney content,
Mattel Shows, progress reports and parental controls.

**Locked entirely without Miko Max:** iHeart Music, DaVinci Games & Shows, and
Lingo Kids.

Miko Max costs from $8.25 a month, or $99 for a year against a $180 list price.

Two things follow. The first is that parental controls and progress reports —
the features a parent is most likely to assume are part of the product — are on
the limited list rather than the free one. The second is that a robot bought at
Christmas and used without Max is still a working robot, which is more than
several competitors can say.

Budget $299 if you want the machine. Budget closer to $400 for the first year
if you want what the advertising shows.

## What it does out of the box

It holds a conversation. Not a scripted exchange of voice commands — you ask it
things, it answers, and it follows up. For a five-year-old that distinction is
the entire product.

It also drives around. Time-of-flight and odometric sensors keep it off the
edge of a table and let it move toward whoever is talking, which is a small
thing that does a lot of work in making a child treat it as a character rather
than a speaker.

Face and voice recognition mean it can tell two children apart and keep their
progress separate. Eight languages are supported: English, both Spanish
variants, Mandarin, Italian, German, French and Arabic. For a bilingual house
that is a genuine reason to prefer it.

## Battery, and the practical shape of a day

About six to seven hours of continuous play, and about four hours to charge
with the supplied 15 W adapter.

Miko recommends sleep mode or shutting it down between sessions, which is a
polite way of saying the battery drains while it sits there waiting. That is
worth knowing before a long car journey.

Wi-Fi is required. Miko 3 does almost nothing without it, so it is not a toy for a
holiday cottage with poor signal, and it is not something to hand a child on a
flight.

## Miko 3 against the Mini

Miko sells three machines and the naming does not help: a Mini, the 3, and a
Max.

The Mini is the cheaper one and it is a different machine rather than a smaller
version of this one. The Max is newer and larger. Anyone comparing them should
read Miko's own comparison rather than ours, because we have reviewed neither
and will not compare specifications we have not read.

What we will say about the range: the 3 is the model with the search demand and
the retail presence behind it, and it is the one sold at Walmart, Target,
Costco and Amazon. That matters for returns and for how easy it is to get help
in two years.

## Who this is for

A child roughly five to ten who talks. That is the real test, more than any
specification — this robot rewards a child who will chat to it and does
comparatively little for one who will not.

It suits a household that already has reliable Wi-Fi and a parent willing to
open an app occasionally. It suits a bilingual family unusually well.

It is also the most defensible purchase in this category for a grandparent
buying a Christmas present, because it does something a parent can point to:
the child talks, the robot answers, and there is a progress report at the end
of it.

## Who should look elsewhere

**Under-fives.** Miko rates it from five and the content assumes some reading
and some conversation. A three-year-old will treat it as a light-up toy at
$299.

**Anyone over about ten.** The ceiling is real and arrives faster than the
price suggests. This is a robot to be outgrown, and the only product on this
site bought with that in mind.

**A home without solid Wi-Fi.** It needs a connection for almost everything.

**Anyone who wants a robot pet.** It is a teacher with a face, not a companion
animal. For company rather than lessons, Moflin and the desk robots are a
different proposition at a different price.

**Anyone allergic to subscriptions.** It works without one and you will feel
the limits. If a recurring charge is a dealbreaker on principle, buy something
whose full feature set is included.

## Buying one, and which colour

Red and Blue, at the same $299, sold as two separate Amazon listings. Our buy
button points at the Red. Nothing else differs.

Miko also sells directly and through Walmart, Target, Costco and others, so it
is worth a price check before ordering — this is a machine that goes on offer.

One year of warranty, thirty days to return it.

## What we cannot tell you

**How long a child stays interested.** Miko publishes a study of its own about
three-month outcomes. We have not run one and would not repeat a
manufacturer's figure as though we had.

**What happens to the content if Miko stops trading.** Almost everything
interesting here runs through Miko's servers, and a company that leaves the
market takes the content with it. That is a real risk in this category and no
maker publishes a plan for it.

**Whether Max is worth $99.** It depends entirely on which apps your child
uses, and you cannot know that before owning the robot. Start without it.
