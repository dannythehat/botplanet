The Makeblock mBot arrives in pieces, and that is what you are buying.

$69, about fifteen minutes of assembly, and a child who has seen the inside of
a robot before being asked to program one. No other machine in this category
does the first half.

## Fifteen minutes with a screwdriver

Chassis, motors, sensor board, wheels. It goes together in roughly a quarter of
an hour.

Short enough that a child finishes it. That is the number that matters — a kit
that takes an afternoon gets abandoned two-thirds through and becomes a bag of
parts on a shelf, and the mBot is deliberately on the right side of that line.

They will also take it apart again, which is fine and is the point.

## What the build teaches that the code does not

You cannot assemble this without noticing that the sensor is a component, the
motor is a component, and a wire runs between them.

Sealed robots hide that. A Sphero is a ball; the relationship between the
program and the machine is a black box a child accepts on faith. Here the
line-follower a program reads is a part they screwed on themselves, and the
connection between the two is obvious rather than magical.

Makeblock's own framing is electronics, machinery and robotics rather than
coding alone, and for once the marketing describes the product accurately.

## Then it is block coding, in Scratch

Drag-and-drop, Scratch-based. Standard, well documented, and the same visual
language most schools already use.

Which means a child who has met Scratch in a computing lesson is not learning a
new environment to drive this — they are pointing something they already know
at hardware they just built. That is a shorter step than any proprietary app.

The ceiling is the same as every block editor: no text programming. For Python
you want the Sphero BOLT.

## mBot2, mBot Ranger, and which one this is

Makeblock sells several. The mBot2 measures 720 searches a month on its own and
the Ranger another 320, so a fair number of people arrive at the name meaning a
different robot.

This page is the original mBot, ASIN B00SK5RUQY, at $69. The mBot2 is newer and
more expensive with a different controller board. The Ranger is a three-form
kit aimed older. We have not read those listings and we do not sell them, so
compare on the model name rather than the brand.

## Who this is for

Eight to twelve, and specifically the child who takes things apart. If yours
has ever unscrewed something they were not supposed to, this is the robot.

Also the cheapest serious entry in the category. $69 against $175 for an Ozobot
Evo, and the gap is the lesson library rather than the hardware.

## Who should look elsewhere

**Anyone who wants it working out of the box.** It is a kit. Somebody has to
build it, and if that somebody is you at eleven at night, buy something else.

**A child who finds loose components stressful rather than inviting.** This is
a real split and it shows up early. A Sphero Mini is $50, sealed, and works in
a minute.

**Under-eights.** Rated 8 to 12, and small screws set the floor there
regardless of coding ability.

**Anyone needing lesson plans.** Makeblock has resources; Ozobot has 700 of
them sorted by subject.
