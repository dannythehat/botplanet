**The short answer.** The Seagull SE is the cheapest machine we cover and the most
honest thing in the budget end of this market: a cordless vacuum that does the floor
of a small above-ground pool for ninety minutes and does nothing else. No walls, no
waterline, no app, no promises. At roughly $150 when we last saw it on sale, it is
the price of a decent pool net with a motor and a mind of its own.

One thing you should know before reading on: one of this machine's two Amazon
listings died while this review was being written, and it was the one our price
checks were reading. The section below explains what happened and why the price on
this page may briefly lag the listing.

---

## Who this is for

A small, flat-bottomed above-ground pool — the Intex-and-Bestway world — where the
job is sand, grit and sunk leaves on the floor, and the budget is a tenth of what
the flagship robots cost. Aiper lists this machine for above-ground pools, and our
research puts its comfortable ceiling at around 33 ft.

It is also the right first robot. If you have been scooping a pool by hand and want
to find out what a robot changes without committing four figures, this is the
cheapest credible way to learn — from the brand that sells more robotic pool
cleaners than anyone else.

---

## Who should look elsewhere

- **Your pool is in-ground with real walls.** This machine does not climb. A
  corded Dolphin Nautilus CC Plus does floors and walls for the mid-range money.
- **The waterline ring is your complaint.** Nothing here touches it.
- **Leaves land on your water faster than they sink.** A surface skimmer like the
  Betta SE Plus attacks that problem at the top, which is where it starts, and
  we compare the solar pool skimmers on a page of their own.
- **You want an app, a schedule, or a map.** There is a power button. That is the
  interface.

## What 90 minutes actually covers

Ninety minutes of runtime, from Aiper's own page — which also says the battery is
80% bigger than earlier Seagull models', with charging time cut by half.

Our artwork rounds that 80% up to "2x". Aiper's published figure is 80%, against
what it calls "similar Seagull models", and that is the number to hold.

Ninety minutes is the right amount for this machine's actual job. A small
above-ground pool is a single room, not a house: a robot that wanders it for an
hour and a half covers the floor several times over. The flagship robots run five
hours because they have walls and waterlines to do. This one does not, and its
battery is sized for the job it has.

---

## Floor only, and why that is fine at this price

Aiper's page lists one cleaning surface: the pool floor. The sides of an
above-ground pool are soft, vertical and shallow — there is no tide-mark tile line,
and most of what ends up in the water finishes on the bottom. Floor-only is not a
compromise in this pool; it is the whole job.

The machine drives a random path — no mapping, no camera, no sonar. In a big
in-ground pool that costs coverage; in a small one, ninety minutes of random walk
covers the floor with room to spare. Navigation intelligence is what you are *not*
paying for, and in this pool you would not collect the benefit anyway.

## Getting it in and out

Drop it in, press the button. At the end of a cycle it parks itself against the
pool wall — that is Aiper's own description — so you are not fishing the middle of
the pool for it. The box includes a retrieval hook that fits a standard pole, and
the machine's handle is shaped for it.

Aiper does not publish a weight for it, which is the one handling fact we would
most like to give you. What we can say is that this is physically the smallest
machine we cover, and the whole point of its class is that one person handles it
casually.

## The sparsest specification sheet we cover

Aiper's product page for this machine is one of the thinnest we have read from any
manufacturer — and we say that having noted the same tendency on their flagship
pages. No weight. No dimensions. No battery capacity. No charge time. No filter
description, let alone a micron rating. No warranty term. No maximum pool size.

Two of those gaps close from other sources: the surviving Amazon listing states a
2.5-hour recharge — the figure our charging artwork carries — and our research puts
the comfortable ceiling at about 33 ft. Both are labelled by source in the table
below, because neither comes from Aiper's own page.

At $150 a sparse sheet is more forgivable than at $1,700. But a filter with no
stated fineness on a machine whose whole job is picking fine grit off a floor is a
genuine gap, not a rounding error.

## The renewed listing, and the 100-minute trap

Alongside the live listing, Amazon also carries a "Renewed" one — refurbished
stock — whose title advertises **100 minutes** of runtime on a "2026" model.
Aiper's own current page says 90.

We do not know whether that ten-minute difference is a genuine model-year revision
or a listing writer's optimism, and neither will you. What we do know: a renewed
unit's warranty position belongs to the refurbisher, not to Aiper, and its price
tells you nothing about the new machine's. Every figure on this page describes the
machine as Aiper's current page sells it — 90 minutes — and none of it is evidence
about what a refurbished unit will do.

## The recall that was not this machine

Aiper has had two products recalled and neither is this one. The relevant one for
you is the **Seagull Pro, model ZT6001**, recalled on 20 March 2025 as CPSC recall
25-187 for an adapter that could overheat — 19 reports of melting, smoking or fire
while charging, five with property damage.

Seagull **Pro**, not Seagull **SE**. One word, two products, several hundred
dollars apart. If you searched "Aiper Seagull recall" and landed here worried, the
answer is that this machine is not in it.

The other recall was the Elite Pro, model GS100, in August 2023. Also not this
machine.

What we will not tell you is that nobody has ever had a problem with a Seagull SE.
The CPSC publishes its recall database in full and we have read it; individual
consumer reports live on SaferProducts.gov and are not available to us as a count
per model. No recall is a fact. No incidents is not a claim we can make.

## The verdict

As a first robot for a small above-ground pool, the Seagull SE is the easiest
recommendation in our catalogue: it costs a tenth of the flagships, does the one
job that pool actually has, and comes from the category's biggest brand. Ninety
minutes of floor cleaning for $150 is not a compromise — it is the correct
product for that pool.

Buy it knowing what you are buying: no walls, no waterline, no app, and no
published filter spec — from a listing whose title says Seagull SE, because its
Amazon listings churn. If your
pool has walls worth climbing or a waterline worth scrubbing, spend more. If it is
a ring of soft-sided water in a garden, spend this.
