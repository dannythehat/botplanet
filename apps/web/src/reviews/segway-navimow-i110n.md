Segway includes the cellular data. That is the sentence to notice on this
listing, because a wire-free mower positioned by satellite needs a correction
signal from somewhere, and the usual answers are an antenna on your roof or a
subscription.

The Navimow i110N takes Network RTK with no local antenna, and Segway states
the access is included at no extra cost with the required cellular data
provided free of charge. At $1,099 for a quarter acre, that is the argument.

## Who should not buy this

Anybody with mature trees over the lawn. This is a satellite-positioned machine
and a canopy defeats satellite positioning — not slowly, not partially, but as
a hard failure with no setting that fixes it. VisionFence helps it avoid things;
it does not tell the mower where it is. Under trees the answer is a
camera-navigated machine: the WORX Landroid Vision at $1,022.54 or the eufy E15
at $1,199.99.

Anybody with a slope past thirty per cent, which is seventeen degrees. That
rules this out for a bank and points at the Dreame A3 AWD 1000 or a Mammotion
LUBA 3 AWD.

## What Network RTK saves you

Centimetre-accurate satellite positioning needs a fixed reference. The old
answer was a physical antenna you mount on a pole or a roof, level, with a clear
view of the sky and a cable run back to the base — an afternoon's work and a
thing that has to stay put.

Network RTK gets the correction over the mobile network instead. Segway's own
words are that access is included at no extra cost and the cellular data is
provided free of charge, which removes the second question people ask, which is
what happens when the free year ends.

What to do with that: check your lawn's view of the sky before anything else.
Stand where the grass is and look up. If you can see most of it, this is a
plug-and-play machine at a fair price. If a large oak is in the way, none of
this applies to you.

## VisionFence, and what it is for

Over 150 objects recognised, across animals, tools and everyday obstacles.

That is obstacle avoidance, not navigation, and the distinction is worth
holding: VisionFence stops the mower hitting the hosepipe, RTK tells it which
part of the lawn it is on. A machine can be excellent at the first and unusable
in your garden because of the second.

## The numbers, and the one Segway does not print

A quarter acre — 10,890 square feet. Thirty per cent slope, which is seventeen
degrees. Cutting height two to 3.6 inches, adjusted by hand rather than
electrically.

Two inches is a high floor. If you cut short — fine fescue, or a lawn you like
scalped — this machine's lowest setting may be above where you want it, and
that is a specification worth checking against your mower's current height
before you replace it.

## What we could not find out

Segway publishes coverage, slope, cutting height, the navigation stack and the
VisionFence object count. It does not publish a cutting width anywhere we could
read on 10 August 2026, nor battery capacity, runtime, charge time, noise, IP
rating, machine weight or warranty length. Recorded as not disclosed.

Worth knowing before you compare: the range refreshed at CES 2026. The i2 AWD
series starts at $999 and the X4 series at $2,499, and the i105N and i110N
remain on sale beneath them. This is the current i-series machine at the size
most American lawns need, but it is no longer the newest thing Segway makes,
and an i2 AWD at $999 covering a slope this one cannot is worth a look before
you buy.

## Who should buy one

Somebody with an open quarter acre, a clear view of the sky, and a dislike of
mounting antennas. Eleven hundred dollars with no subscription and no
installation is a clean proposition and there is nothing wrong with it.

Anybody with trees should buy a camera-navigated mower. Anybody with a bank
should buy all-wheel drive. Anybody who cuts their grass below two inches
should check that figure twice.
