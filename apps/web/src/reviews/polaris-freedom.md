**The short answer.** The Polaris FREEDOM is a genuinely cordless robot for in-ground
pools that cleans the floor, the walls and the waterline, runs for two and a half
hours on a charge, and drops onto a contact dock afterwards with no plug to line up.
There is no cable in the water and none to untangle. It is also one of the most
expensive machines in this category, and the thing you are paying for — the battery —
is the one part of it guaranteed to be worse in five years than it is today.

One number decides whether you should buy it at all, and Polaris publishes that
number in exactly one place: a marketing panel.

---

## Who this is for

An in-ground pool, up to about 50 ft long, where the cord is the thing you actually
hate. Not the cleaning, not the price — the cord. If you have spent a season
unwinding a floating cable, walking a robot back to the shallow end, or fishing a
tangle off the drain, the FREEDOM removes that entire category of annoyance rather
than reducing it.

It also suits someone who wants the waterline handled. Unlike the corded Dolphin
Nautilus CC Plus, which does not scrub the waterline at all, Polaris's own support
page lists this machine's surfaces as floor, wall **and** waterline, and gives it a
dedicated waterline-only mode.

---

## Who should look elsewhere

- **Your pool is above ground.** Polaris lists this as in-ground only. That is a
  flat exclusion, not a preference.
- **Your pool is deeper than 13 ft or shallower than 15 in.** Both limits come
  from the owner's manual and both are hard.
- **You want the cheapest way to get a clean floor.** A corded machine does that
  job for considerably less, forever, with no part that wears out on a calendar —
  the Dolphin Nautilus CC Plus for floors and walls, the BuBlue Bubot 800P if you
  want the waterline too.
- **You keep equipment for a decade.** The battery is a consumable. Plan for
  replacing it, or buy corded.
- **You want the same cordless convenience for less.** The WYBOT C1 covers floors,
  walls and the waterline at around a third of this price; what you give up is the
  bigger battery and the iAquaLink ecosystem.
- **You will not put the charging dock somewhere with Wi-Fi.** The manual requires
  it. More on that below, because nobody mentions it before you buy.

---

## The 50 ft question, and where that number comes from

The single most important thing to know about a pool robot is the biggest pool it
can finish. For the FREEDOM that figure is 50 ft, and we are going to be precise
about its source, because we checked all four.

The owner's manual does not state it. The quick start guide does not state it. The
Polaris support page for SKU FFREEDOM does not state it. All three publish an
operating **depth** range instead — minimum 15 in, maximum 13 ft — and say nothing
at all about length.

The only Polaris source that gives a length is the A+ content on the Amazon listing,
where it appears in display capitals: cordless robotic pool cleaner for in-ground
pools up to 50 ft. That is manufacturer-authored copy rather than a seller's
description, which is why we accept it. It is still marketing artwork, and it is
carrying a constraint that the technical documentation leaves out.

What to do with that. If your pool is comfortably under 50 ft, this is not your
problem. If your pool is 48 ft, or 52 ft, you are making a decision on a figure that
Polaris has never printed in a manual, and we would ask the retailer to confirm it in
writing before buying. We would also rather tell you that than round the number off
and hope.

## What it actually cleans

Floor, walls and waterline. Four modes: floor only, floor and walls and waterline,
waterline only, and a SMART cycle that the app optimises.

The waterline mode matters more than it sounds. A tide-mark at the tile line is the
most visible dirt in a pool and the thing most floor-and-wall robots leave behind
entirely. Having a mode that does nothing else is more useful than having waterline
listed among a machine's capabilities and then averaged into a general cycle.

Polaris publishes an operating power figure of 29.4 W. It does not publish a flow
rate, so there is no suction number here to compare against a rival's suction number.
Anyone quoting one for this machine got it somewhere other than Polaris.

---

## Cordless, and what that actually costs

The battery is 9.6 Ah at 29.4 V DC, and it is the reason this machine exists. It is
also the reason it will not last like a corded one.

Lithium-ion cells lose capacity with cycles and with heat, and a pool robot gets
both. Nobody publishes a cycle life for this pack, us included — see the gaps
section. What is predictable is the direction: the runtime you get in year four will
be shorter than the runtime you get in week one, and at some point the answer is a
new battery rather than a new schedule.

That is not a reason to avoid it. It is a reason to price it honestly. A corded
robot has no part that degrades on a calendar; this one does, and the convenience is
what you are buying with that trade.

---

## Runtime, and two figures that are the same figure

The quick start guide gives two runtimes: 2 h 30 for floor and walls, 1 h 30 for
floor only. The Amazon A+ panel says "cleans for up to 2.5 hours".

Those agree. Two and a half hours is 2 h 30, and it is the longest mode rather than
a typical one. We are spelling that out because a spec sheet listing "2.5 hours"
beside a quick start guide listing "2h30" looks like two sources disagreeing, and it
is one source stated twice.

Charging is four hours on the Easy-Charge dock. Polaris also says "under five hours"
and "less than five hours" in two other places. Those are a ceiling rather than a
rival figure — four falls inside five — and Polaris prints both itself, so we have
not reconciled anything here that Polaris had not already.

---

## Filtration, and the rating nobody publishes

A 4 L all-purpose canister, accessed from the top.

Polaris does not publish a micron rating for it. Not on the support page, not in the
manual. So a claim that this machine filters to any particular fineness has no
manufacturer source behind it, and you should treat a retailer quoting one as
quoting themselves.

Four litres is a large canister for this class, which matters in practice more than
a micron figure does: capacity is what decides whether you empty it every cycle or
every third one.

---

## The app, and the Wi-Fi requirement nobody mentions before you buy

Control is iAquaLink, which is Fluidra's own platform rather than a white-labelled
one, and it handles mode selection, battery status and cycle-complete alerts. There
is no handset in the box. Control is the app or a slider on the unit itself.

Here is the part worth reading twice. The owner's manual requires the **charging
location** to have adequate Wi-Fi strength. Not the pool — the place the dock sits.
If your router is at the front of the house and your dock is going in a pump shed at
the far end of the garden, you need to solve that before the robot arrives, not
after. It is a genuine installation constraint and it appears in a manual almost
nobody reads before purchase.

## Weight, handling and getting it out

Twenty pounds — 9.1 kg — for the cleaner itself. The manual states it plainly. The
packed weight is 33 lb and is a shipping figure, not a lifting one.

**A correction, in the paragraph where the mistake lived.** Our own product record
for this machine said the manufacturer does not publish a weight. That was wrong.
Polaris publishes it in the owner's manual, in the specification table, and we did
not read far enough before writing that line. Corrected on 4 August 2026.

Twenty pounds is a two-handed lift out of water, and water adds to it. A removal
hook is supplied, and the machine climbs to the surface at the end of a cycle so you
are lifting it from the edge rather than reaching down for it. The unit measures
16 × 16.5 × 11 in, so it is compact rather than light.

## What is in the box, and the warranty we cannot confirm

The manual lists three things: the cleaner, the charging station, and a removal hook.

The warranty is the honest gap on this page. Polaris references a Limited Warranty
in the manual — inside an exclusion clause — and never states its term. The support
page states none. The A+ panels state none.

**A second correction.** Our own product record claimed a two-year limited warranty
for this machine. We cannot show you where that figure came from, and we could not
find it in any Polaris source on rechecking, so it has been removed rather than left
standing. Corrected on 4 August 2026. If you are buying partly on warranty length,
get it in writing from the retailer before you pay.

## The name trap

Polaris sells the FREEDOM, the FREEDOM SC, the FREEDOM LT and the FREEDOM Plus.
Everything on this page describes the base FREEDOM, SKU **FFREEDOM**, also carrying
the reference FR550CBR.

The chassis designation in the manual is TYPE EB37 — and that code is shared with
the FREEDOM Plus. So if you are checking a listing against a manual, the chassis code
will not tell you which machine you are looking at. The SKU will.

This is not pedantry. Sibling models in the same family are where robot buying goes
wrong most often: the price looks close, the name looks right, and the specification
is somebody else's.

## What we cannot tell you

Written from published sources and no pool. Specifically:

- **Cycle life of the battery.** No source publishes one. We know it degrades; we
  cannot tell you over how many cycles.
- **Replacement battery cost and availability.** Not published where we could find it.
- **Flow rate.** Operating power is published; suction is not.
- **Micron rating.** Not published.
- **Warranty term.** Not published, as above.
- **Compatible surfaces.** The manual warns about vinyl liner patterns and does not
  enumerate which finishes are fine. If you have a painted or an unusual finish,
  ask before buying.
- **How well the waterline mode actually works.** Polaris lists the capability and
  we accept the listing. Nobody here has watched it run.

That list is longer than we would like. It is also the whole list, which is more than
most reviews of this machine will give you.

---

## The verdict

The FREEDOM is the machine to buy if the cord is the problem and the budget can carry
it. It cleans more surfaces than the corded mid-range — waterline included — and it
removes the single most irritating thing about owning a pool robot.

What it does not do is escape physics. You are buying a wear part, at a premium, and
the manufacturer has left more of its specification unpublished than we are
comfortable with: no warranty term, no micron rating, no flow rate, and the pool-size
limit stated only in marketing artwork.

If the cord genuinely bothers you, buy it and plan for a battery. If it does not, a
corded machine will clean your pool for less money and for longer, and you will not
be reading a marketing panel to find out how long your pool is allowed to be.
