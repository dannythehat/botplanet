A four-year-old programs the Sphero indi by putting coloured cards on the
floor.

No screen. No account. No app, unless you want one. It is one of three
screen-free robots in this category — Botley and the Code and Go Robot Mouse
are the other two — and $100 is what it costs.

## How a card becomes a program

Lay the cards down. indi drives, reads the colour under it, and does what that
colour means — turn, speed up, stop, celebrate.

Green is go faster. Pink turns right. The child arranges a route, watches the
robot take it, and changes a card when it goes wrong. That is a program: a
sequence, an outcome, and a debug.

Nobody has to explain any of this. A three-year-old works it out by watching
once. Botley and the Code and Go Robot Mouse are the only other robots on this
site sold as screen-free.

## Screen-free is the actual product

Sphero sells a simplified drag-and-drop app for indi and it is optional.

The reason to buy this over a $50 Sphero Mini is not capability — the Mini does
far more. It is that the Mini's entire experience lives inside a tablet, and a
four-year-old with a tablet is doing something other than what you bought the
robot for.

Here the tablet stays in a drawer. The floor is the interface.

## What it teaches, honestly stated

Cause and effect. Pattern recognition. Colours and directions.

Not syntax, not logic gates, not loops. The claim on the box is early-years
reasoning and that claim is accurate, which is rarer in this category than it
should be.

The best thing children do with it is unplanned: they stop building routes for
the robot and start building mazes it has to solve. That is a different and
better exercise, and it arrives on its own.

## The ceiling is real and Sphero admits it

The age field on the listing reads 48 to 144 months.

Twelve years is generous. In practice the colour-card idea runs out somewhere
around seven, because a child who can read can write instructions more precise
than a pink tile. When that happens the answer is block coding on a Sphero
Mini, and the money you spent here has done its job.

Buy this knowing it is a two- or three-year robot, not a decade of one.

## Who this is for

Four to about seven. A first robot, a first idea of what a program is, and a
household already tired of screens.

Also a sibling situation: it is heavy enough at 0.56 kg and simple enough that a
younger child can play with it while an older one uses something harder.

## Who should look elsewhere

**Anyone over about seven.** They will be bored, and they should be. The
Ozobot Evo starts screen-free with marker-pen colour codes and then keeps
going into a block editor.

**Anyone wanting the child to reach real code.** This has no path to text
programming. The Sphero BOLT does, at $179.

**A classroom.** Sphero sells an indi class pack with code mats and literacy
cards. Our buy button points at the At-Home Learning Kit, ASIN B094X6TV5V, and
one robot is not a lesson for thirty.

## What we cannot tell you

**How long the interest lasts.** The honest ceiling is the age, and Sphero's
own field says 144 months while every parent account we can find says
something much lower. We have no measurement.

**Whether the silicone survives a toddler.** Plastic and silicone is the
material list; there is no drop rating anywhere.

**What the class pack adds.** Different product, different price, not read.
