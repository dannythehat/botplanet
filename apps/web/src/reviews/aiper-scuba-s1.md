**The short answer.** The Scuba S1 is Aiper's mid-range machine and the quiet
all-rounder of our catalogue: cordless, wall-climbing, waterline-scrubbing, with the
same 3-micron filtration as Aiper's flagships and a four-zone coverage claim that
includes the one place most robots give up — the shallow ledge. Aiper calls it a
best-seller and backs the claim with a number: half a million units worldwide.

Two honest wrinkles before you buy. Aiper's own page disagrees with itself about the
runtime and about which pools this machine is for. And the only new-condition Amazon
listing for it fails our identity checks, so the buy button on this page goes to a
search, not a listing. Both get their own sections.

---

## Who this is for

An in-ground pool up to 50 ft — Aiper's stated ceiling, alongside a 1,600 sq ft
area — where you want the floor, the walls, the waterline and the shallow ledge
handled by one cordless machine at a mid-range price. In our catalogue it sits
squarely between the $150 Seagull SE, which does floors only, and the $849 Scuba
V3, which adds the camera.

It also suits the pool with a tanning ledge or long shallow steps. Aiper claims
cleaning in water as shallow as 12 inches, and that is genuinely uncommon — most
robots simply beach themselves there.

## Who should look elsewhere

- **Your pool runs longer than 50 ft.** The Aiper Scuba X1 Pro Max carries a
  100 ft rating; the Polaris FREEDOM carries 50 with a bigger battery.
- **You want the surface skimmed.** Nothing here touches floating debris — that is
  the X1 Pro Max's party trick, or a Betta SE Plus alongside.
- **You want a camera choosing where to clean.** That is the Scuba V3.
- **Your pool is above ground.** Maybe — but read the pool-type section first,
  because Aiper's own page points both ways.

## The correction: our own records under-sold this machine

Until today, our catalogue said the Scuba S1 cleans floors and walls. Aiper's own
product page says more, plainly: "4-Zone Full Coverage Cleaning — Shallow Areas,
Waterline, Walls, Floors." Our verification record has held those words since 31
July; the catalogue row that drives BotMatch never caught up, and we fixed it the
day this review was written.

The error was in the machine's favour to fix. A buyer whose complaint is the greasy
tide-mark at the tile line asks BotMatch for waterline cleaning, and until today
BotMatch would not have offered this machine at all — steering a mid-range budget
toward pricier robots for a job this one claims to do. Same class of mistake we
found on the Dolphin Nautilus CC Plus, pointing the other way: that catalogue row
promised a waterline the machine does not clean. Both are why every claim on these
pages now traces to a manufacturer sentence.

## Four zones, including the one robots skip

Floors, walls, waterline — and shallow areas, which Aiper defines with a number: as
little as 12 inches of water.

The shallow claim is the interesting one. A robot needs water over its intakes to
grip and to move; a tanning ledge or a wide top step defeats most of them, and
owners end up brushing the one part of the pool they can already stand in. If the
S1 genuinely works a 12-inch ledge — and we have not tested it — that is a real
differentiator at this price, and it is Aiper's own printed claim rather than a
reviewer's enthusiasm.

Navigation is Aiper's WavePath 2.0 — planned paths rather than a random walk, no
camera, no sonar mapping. Between the V3's camera and the Seagull's randomness,
this is the sensible middle.

## The runtime Aiper disagrees with itself about

Aiper's specification block says the battery lasts **up to 180 minutes**. Aiper's
own marketing copy, one scroll earlier on the same page, describes the machine
dedicating its energy across "the full **150 minutes** of operation". Our artwork
carries the 150 figure.

Both numbers are Aiper's. The likeliest reading is that 180 is the ceiling in an
eco-style mode and 150 is a full-power figure, but the page does not say so, and we
will not pretend it does. Either number comfortably covers a 50 ft pool; just do
not buy this machine on the strength of the bigger one. Charging is stated as 3 to
4 hours — a range, and we quote it as one.

---

## Suction and filtration: the flagship parts, downsized

4,200 GPH of suction, and the same two-layer filter arrangement as Aiper's
flagships: a 180-micron basket for leaves and grit in front of a 3-micron MicroMesh
ultra-fine layer, in a 3.5-litre basket.

That 3-micron figure is the finest published rating in our catalogue, shared across
Aiper's Scuba range — and the S1 is the cheapest machine that carries it. The usual
context applies: it is the manufacturer's rating for one layer, not a lab result.
The 3.5-litre basket is mid-sized — smaller than the X1 Pro Max's five litres,
bigger than anything the budget class publishes, which is nothing.

---

## Five modes and the weekly plan

Schedule, Wall, Floor, Automatic and Eco — five modes, selected from the app, which
also carries cleaning history and over-the-air updates. The schedule runs a weekly
custom plan; Aiper's page frames it as 45-minute sessions up to every 48 hours.

The honest note on scheduling a cordless machine: a schedule is only as good as the
charge in the battery, and this one recharges on a wall charger, not a dock. A
weekly plan means remembering to put it back on charge — which is the one part of
"set and forget" that stays manual on every machine in this class except the
dock-charging V3 and X1 Pro Max.

## In-ground or above-ground? Aiper's page points both ways

The specification block on Aiper's page lists the ideal pool type as **in-ground**.
Marketing copy on the same page says it is "designed for both above-ground and
in-ground pools". Both sentences are Aiper's, on one page, and they do not agree.

Our catalogue lists it for both, following the broader claim, and a soft-sided
above-ground pool is mechanically an easier job than a tiled in-ground one. But if
you are buying it specifically for an above-ground pool, know that the spec table
does not back you — only the copywriter does. We flag it because nobody else will.

## The machine Aiper was ordered to hand out

On 20 March 2025 the CPSC recalled the Aiper Seagull Pro, model ZT6001, over an
adapter that could overheat: 19 reports of melting, smoking or fire while
charging, five with property damage, no injuries. The remedy the Commission
published is specific, and it is this machine — recalled Seagull Pro owners get a
free Scuba S1 and a prepaid label.

That is worth knowing for two reasons. This machine is not recalled, and a fair
number of the Scuba S1s in American pools this season arrived as replacements for
one that was.

Aiper's earlier recall, the Elite Pro model GS100 in August 2023, was also not
this machine. Both faults were on the mains side — a cord used without its
adapter, then an adapter on its own — rather than a cell failing in the water.

We are not telling you nobody has ever reported a problem with a Scuba S1. The
recall database is public and complete and holds nothing against it; the consumer
incident database is not something we can query per model, so we are not going to
pretend to have.

## The verdict

On Aiper's own claims, the Scuba S1 is the best-balanced machine in our catalogue's
middle: four zones including the shallow ledge, flagship filtration, real modes and
a real app, at a mid-range price from the category's biggest brand. Half a million
units sold is not proof it is good — but it is proof the claims have been tested by
more owners than any reviewer will ever match.

What keeps it from an unreserved recommendation is sourcing, not engineering:
Aiper's page disagrees with itself twice, the only Amazon listing flunks basic
identity checks, and the warranty is a blank. None of that is the machine's fault.
All of it is worth knowing before your money moves.
