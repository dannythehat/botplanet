**The short answer.** The Scuba X1 Pro Max sits near the top of what we cover
and is the only one that genuinely does all four jobs: it skims the surface, scrubs the
waterline, climbs the walls and vacuums the floor, mapping the pool with ultrasound
as it goes. Aiper backs it with a three-year warranty — the longest stated term in
our catalogue, matched only by the Beatbot, which backs its three years with
replacement rather than repair — and publishes more engineering detail than any rival we have read.

At $1,700 it costs twice what the camera-guided Scuba V3 does. Whether the second
thousand dollars is worth it comes down to one question: do you want one machine
doing the skimmer's job as well as the cleaner's?

---

## Who this is for

A large in-ground pool — our catalogue holds a 100 ft rating from owner research —
whose owner wants the whole job done by one machine: surface debris before it sinks,
the tide-mark at the tile line, the walls, and the floor. Run separately, that is a
skimmer plus a cleaner; this does both, and parks itself at the surface when it is
done.

It also suits a salt pool owner thinking long-term. Aiper publishes an unusually
frank durability sheet for this machine — tested to 50,000 ppm salinity, a design
lifespan of ten years, and that three-year warranty in writing.

---

## Who should look elsewhere

- **Your budget is under four figures.** The Scuba V3 does floor, walls and
  waterline with a camera for half the price. It does not skim the surface; that is
  most of what the extra money buys here.
- **Your pool already has a skimmer you like.** Then you are paying the flagship
  premium for a job that is already done — a Betta SE Plus alongside a cheaper
  cleaner covers the same ground for far less.
- **Your pool is above ground.** Aiper lists this for in-ground pools.
- **You want the lightest possible lift.** This is a big machine. Aiper publishes
  no weight for it, and the handling section explains why that gap matters at this
  size.

## What the second thousand dollars buys

Against its own stablemate, the $849 Scuba V3, the Pro Max adds:

- **Surface skimming.** The V3 never touches floating debris; this hoovers it up
  before it sinks, in a skim mode Amazon's listing rates at up to twelve hours.
- **Ultrasonic mapping.** OmniSense+ 2.0 builds a map of the pool and plans
  coverage; the V3 reacts to what its camera sees ahead of it.
- **Raw power.** 8,500 GPH through a dual-jet system driven by nine brushless
  motors, against the V3's 4,800.
- **A stated 3-year warranty.** The V3 has no published term at all.
- **Scale.** The V3 is rated by area with no length figure; the catalogue holds
  100 ft for this machine.

What it gives up: the camera. This machine maps with ultrasound and avoids
obstacles, but it does not recognise a drift of sand and drive to it the way the V3
does. Aiper's range splits the two ideas, and the flagship got the sonar.

---

## All four surfaces, and what that actually means

Aiper's listing bullet is explicit: floor, walls, waterline, and the water surface.
The machine is sold as a robot vacuum *and* a robotic pool skimmer, and that second
noun is the unusual one — no other machine in our catalogue except the Betta
touches the surface at all, and the Betta does nothing else.

Debris caught on the surface never sinks, never stains, and never needs vacuuming
off the bottom later. A cleaner that skims is eating its own future workload. When
it finishes, Smart Surface Parking floats it to the edge for collection — no hook,
no reaching into the deep end.

---

## Suction: 8,500 GPH and nine motors

The headline suction figure is Aiper's, printed in the listing title, and it is the
largest in our catalogue by some distance — the V3 claims 4,800, the Nautilus 4,500.
Nine brushless motors drive the dual-jet system, per Aiper's own page.

The usual caveat applies to all three numbers: gallons-per-hour is a manufacturer's
pump rating, not an independent measurement of cleaning, and no lab we know of has
put these machines side by side. What a bigger number reliably buys is headroom for
heavy debris — sand, stones, acorns — which is exactly what Amazon's bullet claims
for it.

## The mode times, decoded

Our artwork on this page lists three durations: 12-hour surface cleaning, 5-hour
floor cleaning, and a 5.5-hour "SuperEco" mode. The numbers are all real; two of the
labels need straightening out.

Amazon's listing gives the modes as: **Skim Mode, up to 12 hours** for the surface;
**Eco Mode, up to 5 hours** for standard cleans; and its own efficiency line rates
floor cleaning at **up to 5.5 hours**. So 12, 5 and 5.5 are all Aiper's figures —
but there is no mode called SuperEco on either source we read, and the 5.5-hour
figure belongs to floor cleaning rather than to an eco setting.

The practical read: a full charge is a working day of surface duty or an afternoon
of hard floor cleaning, which is comfortably the longest-legged machine we list.

---

## Filtration: the same 3-micron story, doubled

A replaceable 3-micron ultra-fine filter paired with a 180-micron standard mesh —
the same dual-layer arrangement as the Scuba V3, in a five-litre basket.

Three microns is the finest published rating in our catalogue, shared by these two
Aipers, and the same context applies here as there: it is the manufacturer's figure
for one layer, not a lab result. The five-litre capacity is the number that matters
day to day on a big pool — it is what decides whether you empty it every cycle.

## The bundle trap, and a lesson our own records learned

Three ASINs currently sell under this machine's name: the bare Pro Max in grey —
the one this page describes — a bundle with a caddy, and a bundle with Aiper's
HydroComm Pure water monitor. Different contents, different prices, one product
family.

It gets worse. Aiper also sells a listing whose URL reads "Scuba-X1-Pro" but whose
own product fields name it the **Scuba X1+Hy Pro** — the base X1 bundled with a
HydroComm monitor, not a Pro at all. Our own product register accepted that listing
once, on the strength of its URL, and had to be corrected when the fields were
actually read. If a register built specifically to catch this got caught, a shopper
skimming a search page has no chance.

The rule that survives: read the model name in the listing's own details table,
never the URL and never the title. Ours says, in full, "Scuba X1 Pro Max".

## Warranty and durability, in writing

Three years, stated on Aiper's own page — level with the Beatbot for the longest
published term in our catalogue, against one year for the Nautilus and the Betta,
and no stated term at all for the Polaris FREEDOM or the Scuba V3. The Beatbot's
three years promise replacement rather than repair, which is the part this does
not match.

Around it, Aiper publishes an unusually specific durability sheet: a ten-year
design lifespan, salt tested to 50,000 ppm, chlorine to 40 ppm, temperature tested
from -4°F to 158°F, and a million component test cycles. Those are the
manufacturer's own test claims, not ours — but publishing them at all is more than
most of this market does, and a three-year term suggests Aiper believes them.

---

## Not recalled, and the storage rule that protects the outlay

Neither of Aiper's two recalls touches this machine. They were the Elite Pro,
model GS100, in August 2023, and the Seagull Pro, model ZT6001, in March 2025 —
both charging faults, both on the mains side rather than a cell failing in water.
The CPSC recall database holds no other Aiper recall.

We can say no recall because the database is public and we read it. We cannot say
no incidents: the consumer report database is not queryable per model, and a claim
we cannot check is not one we will print.

The part that matters at this price is what happens in the nine months it is not
in the pool. Aiper's own instruction is 40-60 percent charge every two months, in
a cool, well-ventilated place, and never charging in direct sunlight. On a
machine costing this much, with a 3-year warranty that is the strongest here, a
cell left full in a hot shed all winter is the cheapest mistake available and the
most expensive one to discover.

## What we cannot tell you

Written from published sources and no pool:

- **Weight.** Not stated by Aiper or the listing. For a machine this large that is
  a real gap — it decides whether one person lifts it comfortably.
- **The 40-sensor figure.** Our artwork carries it; neither Aiper's page nor the
  listing we read states a sensor count. Treat it as unverified until Aiper prints
  it somewhere checkable.
- **The 100 ft pool rating.** Owner research; Amazon says only "for all in-ground
  pools", and Aiper's page states no length.
- **Charge time.** Not stated on either source we read.
- **Whether mapping beats a camera in practice.** The V3 sees, this one maps.
  Nobody has raced them.

## The verdict

If you want one machine to do everything a pool needs — surface to floor — this is
the only robot in our catalogue that honestly claims the whole job, and it pairs the
claim with the longest warranty and the frankest durability data we have seen from
any maker here.

If your pool already has working surface skimming, think harder. The Scuba V3 gets
you the floor, walls and waterline with smarter navigation for half the money, and
the difference funds a Betta skimmer with change to spare.

Either way, buy it from the listing whose details table says "Scuba X1 Pro Max" in
full. Two bundles and one impostor share its name, and the impostor fooled us
before we learned to read past the URL.
