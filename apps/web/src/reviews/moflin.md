A Moflin is a 260-gram ball of long fur that fits in two hands. It moves its
head. It makes sounds. It has no legs, no wheels and no words, and it will
never fetch anything, answer anything or clean anything.

Casio charges $429 for it.

That combination is why most people arrive here, and it deserves a straight
answer rather than a shrug about emotional technology.

## What Moflin actually does

Casio publishes a specification table for this product, which is more than most
makers in this category manage, so the answer is not a matter of opinion.

Movement is two axes: head rotation and tilt. Audio is one speaker and one
microphone. It carries four kinds of sensor — a microphone, an illuminance
sensor, touch sensors, and an accelerometer and gyroscope pair — and it uses
them to work out how it is being handled, how much light is around it, and
whether the voice it can hear is one it recognises.

Out of that it builds a response to you specifically. Handle it often and
gently and it settles; leave it alone and it does not. Casio describes the
underlying system as allowing four million emotional states, which is a number
about the software rather than a number you can check.

The MofLife app does the housekeeping: naming it, reading a journal of how you
have interacted, setting the volume, checking the battery and pushing firmware
updates.

## It does not walk, and the specification says so

Two of the most common questions about this product are whether a Moflin walks
and whether it talks. Both answers are no, and both are in Casio's own table
rather than hidden.

The Movement row reads two axes, head rotation and tilt. There is nothing else.
It cannot cross a table, follow you between rooms or right itself if it ends up
somewhere awkward. The sounds it makes are sounds, not speech.

None of that is a fault, and treating it as one misreads the product. A machine
that stayed still and made noises would be a toy. A machine that walked would
invite comparison with a dog and lose. Moflin sits deliberately in the gap: it
responds to being held, and holding it is the entire interaction.

If that sounds thin, it is the honest description, and it is the thing to decide
about before the price.

## Why a Moflin costs $429

The parts do not explain it. A 1,200 mAh battery, one speaker, one microphone,
four sensor types and a two-axis head are not $429 of components.

What you are paying for is the behaviour built on top of them, and the fur. The
first is real engineering that does not show up on a spec sheet; the second is
most of why people describe holding one as convincing. Reviewers who have lived
with a Moflin for weeks tend to arrive at the same place — sceptical about the
price, and reluctant to put it down.

Whether that is worth it is a judgement about what you want, not a
specification you can check. Our position: at this price it is a considered
purchase for someone who already knows they want something to look after, and a
poor impulse buy for anyone hoping to be talked into it.

## Battery life, and what happens when it runs out

About five hours of use on a full charge, and about three hours thirty minutes
to charge again. Casio measures both at 25 °C, or 77 °F.

Power draw is roughly 4.7 W.

The useful detail is what happens during those three and a half hours: it
charges in its bed and stays responsive while it does. A pet that switches off
for a third of the day would break the illusion the whole product depends on,
so Casio did not build one.

## No subscription, and that is worth saying plainly

Casio publishes no subscription for Moflin in the US. There is no monthly fee,
no service tier, and no feature that stops working if you decline to pay
something.

That is unusual enough in this category to be a genuine reason to prefer it.
Miko charges for its content tier. Vector's cloud features have been through
one owner's bankruptcy and another's paid plan. ElliQ is sold as a subscription
outright. A companion robot that is bought once and then simply owned is the
exception here.

The warranty runs one year from the day it arrives.

## Who this is for

Someone who wants something to look after in a home where an animal is not
possible. A rented flat with a no-pets clause. An allergy. A care setting. A
schedule that will not carry a dog and should not pretend otherwise.

It also works as a gift for an older relative, and this is the use we would
back most confidently. It weighs 260 grams, needs no training, makes no mess
and cannot be tripped over. Nothing about it demands a manual.

## Who should look elsewhere

**Anyone who wants it to move.** It stays where you put it. If the appeal is a
robot that follows you around a room, this is the wrong product and no amount
of software will change it.

**Anyone who wants a conversation.** It makes sounds. For talking, the desk
companions are a different category and a fifth of the price.

**Anyone buying for a young child.** Casio does not sell it as a toy, the fur
needs brushing with a soft brush rather than handling roughly, and $429 is a
great deal of money to hand to somebody who will test whether it bounces.

**Anyone who wants to try the idea cheaply.** Joy For All sells a realistic
animatronic cat at about a third of this, and for the care-home and dementia
use in particular it is the better first purchase.

## Moflin against aibo and Ropet

Two comparisons come up constantly and both deserve an honest answer.

**Sony aibo** is the robot dog people mean when they ask. It walks, it
recognises faces, and it costs several times a Moflin. It is also not sold on
Amazon in the US, which makes it a considerably harder thing to buy than its
fame suggests. If you want a robot that moves around your house, aibo is the
product — Moflin is not competing for that buyer.

**Ropet** is the newer one, an interactive robot pet at around $299 with a
screen face rather than fur. It is closer to Moflin in intent than aibo is. We
have not reviewed it and will not compare specifications we have not read.

## Where to buy one, and what to check

Casio sells it directly, and it is on Amazon in the US where our buy button
points. The listing we hold is the Silver; Casio also sells a Gold, and the two
differ in colour only.

One thing to check before ordering: the box should contain the Moflin, its bed,
an AC adapter and the startup guide. The bed is the charger, so a listing that
does not mention it is either incomplete or not the full product.

## What we cannot tell you

**How long one lasts.** Moflin has not been on sale in the US long enough for
anybody to answer that honestly, the fur is the part most likely to show wear,
and Casio's warranty is one year rather than a statement about lifespan.

**What repairs cost.** Casio offers support and publishes no repair pricing we
could find.

**Whether the attachment holds.** Every long-term account we have read describes
becoming fond of it. None of them is a year old yet.
