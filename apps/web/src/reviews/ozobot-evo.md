Draw a line in felt-tip and the Ozobot Evo follows it. Draw a short sequence of
coloured dashes across that line and it obeys them as instructions.

That is the trick, it costs $175, and the thing worth paying for is what sits
behind it: over 700 free lessons.

## Colour codes on paper, then a block editor

The Evo Entry Kit ships with the markers. A child draws a track on printer
paper, adds a blue-black-blue code, and the robot speeds up when it crosses it.

There are dozens of those codes — turns, spins, timers, direction changes at a
junction. A program written this way is a drawing, which for a five-year-old is
a far shorter route into the idea than a tablet.

Then it moves into Ozobot Blockly, a block coding editor with five skill
levels running from beginner to master. Same robot, same afternoon, different
ceiling.

## The 700 lessons are the reason to buy it over a toy

Free, and organised — STEAM, computer science, and lessons pinned to ordinary
school subjects.

Every robot in this category claims an educational library. Most of them mean
twelve activity cards. Ozobot's is built for teachers with a scheme of work to
fill, which means somebody has already answered the question you will hit in
month six: what do we do with it now.

That question is where these robots die. A shelf of lesson plans is the most
honest defence against it anyone in this category offers.

## It is built for a classroom and that shows

8.5 × 6.5 × 1.8 inches for the kit, 0.44 kg, sold as classroom-ready.

Durable in the way school equipment is durable — designed to be dropped by
somebody else's child. At a kitchen table that is simply a robot that survives.

The trade is that the marketing, the packaging and the lesson library all
address a teacher. A parent buying one is using a school product at home, which
works fine and occasionally feels like it.

## Amazon says "Toddler" and Amazon is wrong

The listing's own age field reads Toddler. Its title says 5 to 11, and so does
Ozobot.

Believe the title. A toddler cannot draw a straight line in marker pen, let
alone a three-dash colour code, and buying this for a three-year-old will end
with an expensive robot in a drawer. The Sphero indi is the screen-free option
that genuinely works at four.

We are naming the contradiction rather than picking the flattering number,
because it is the kind of field that gets copied across a dozen sites without
anybody opening the box.

## Who this is for

Five to eleven. Ideally an adult who wants a plan rather than a present —
teacher, tutor, or a parent happy to run a lesson.

Also the household where a screen is the argument. The first weeks here need no
device at all.

## Who should look elsewhere

**Anyone aiming at real text programming.** Evo's ceiling is Blockly. The
Sphero BOLT goes to Python and JavaScript for $4 more.

**Under-fives.** The colour codes need fine motor control the age does not
have.

**Anyone who wants a robot rather than a curriculum.** At $175 you are paying
for the lesson library as much as the hardware, and if nobody is going to open
it, the Makeblock mBot does the coding half for $69.

## What we cannot tell you

**Battery life.** No runtime figure appears on the listing, in the title, or in
the specification table.

**How many of the 700 lessons a home user can actually use.** Many assume a
class, a projector and forty minutes. Ozobot does not split the library by
setting.

**What is in the box beyond the robot and markers.** The listing says
"accessories" and does not enumerate them.
