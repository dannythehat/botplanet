One machine in our robot vacuum catalogue has a maker who makes a claim about
deep pile. This is it, and that single fact is most of the case for spending
thirteen hundred dollars.

Roborock states that the Saros 10 "elevates the chassis up to 0.39 inches
(10mm) on high-pile carpets, reducing suction port blockages from long fibers",
and that the mop "automatically detaches in modes where mopping is not needed,
such as Vacuum Only and Vacuum Carpets First". Lifting the whole body rather
than just the pads, and taking the mop off entirely rather than raising it, are
different engineering from everything below it in the range.

## Who should not buy this

Almost everybody. At $1,299.99 this is the most expensive machine in our
catalogue by three hundred and fifty dollars, and for a house of hard floor
and ordinary carpet it does nothing the $449.99 eufy X10 Pro Omni does not
also do.

The reason to buy it is deep or shag pile, and the reason not to is anything
else. If your floors are wood and tile with a couple of rugs, you are being
sold navigation hardware you will never see working.

## The 3.14 inches, which is the other real argument

Three and an eighth inches tall. That is low enough to go under a good deal of
furniture that stops a conventional robot dead, and it is the specification
roborock leads with after suction.

What to do with that: measure the gap under your sofa and under the kick
boards in your kitchen before you decide it matters. A robot that cannot get
under the sofa is a robot that leaves a rectangle of dust in the middle of the
room, and no amount of suction fixes it. If your gaps are over four inches this
is not worth paying for.

## Navigation

ReactiveAI 3.0, which roborock describes as "front triple structured light, an
RGB camera, and VertiBeam lateral structured light". Three sensing methods
rather than one.

Twenty-two thousand Pascals of suction, hot water mop washing, and a RockDock
Ultra base that empties the bin into a bag. The dock is the same idea as
everything else in this bracket; the sideways-looking beam is not, and it is
what lets the machine judge a wall and a chair leg rather than only what is in
front of it.

Obstacle avoidance is most of the price gap between a cheap robot vacuum and
an expensive one, and this is the most of it that money currently buys. On a
floor that is genuinely clear you will never see it working.

## What we could not find out

Roborock publishes a great deal about this machine and still not everything.
Runtime, bin volume, water tank capacity, noise and weight were not on any
page we read, and they are recorded as not disclosed rather than filled in from
elsewhere.

There is a second Amazon listing for the same model, B0DLH45139, carrying no
price and 94 ratings against the pinned listing's 4,428. We cannot tell you
what that listing is — a lapsed seller, a regional variant, a bundle — only
that it exists and that it is not the one we pinned.

## Who should buy one

Somebody with deep or shag pile carpet who has tried a cheaper robot vacuum
and watched it beach. That is a real person, it is a small number of people,
and this is the only machine in our catalogue whose manufacturer says anything
about their floor at all.

Somebody with low furniture and a persistent dust rectangle in the middle of
a room has the second-best reason. Everyone else is looking at a machine that
costs three times what their house needs.
