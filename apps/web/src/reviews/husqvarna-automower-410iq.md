Husqvarna publishes two working-area figures for this machine and everybody
else in the category publishes one. Half an acre if your lawn is a sensible
shape, a quarter acre if it is not.

That is the most honest line on any lawn mower spec sheet we read on 10 August
2026, and it is the reason to take the 410iQ seriously at $2,499.99 when
cheaper machines claim the same half acre without qualifying it.

## Who should not buy this

Anyone whose main problem is a steep bank. Forty-five per cent inside the
mowing area sounds generous until you read the second number: fifteen per cent
at the boundary. A slope the machine can cut is not necessarily a slope it can
edge, and the Mammotion LUBA 3 AWD and the Dreame A3 AWD both do eighty per
cent on all four wheels.

Anyone on a tight budget. Two and a half thousand dollars buys the WORX
Landroid Vision at $1,022.54 and a great deal of change, and the WORX covers
the same half acre.

## What the two area figures actually tell you

Half an acre "systematically", a quarter acre "in irregular patterns", plus or
minus twenty per cent.

Every robot mower's rated area assumes it can drive long straight lines. Put it
in a garden of narrow strips, island beds and a path down the middle and it
spends its battery turning. Husqvarna is the only maker here that says so, and
what to do with it is simple: if your lawn is one open rectangle, read the half
acre. If it is three odd shapes joined by gaps, read the quarter, and then
check that against the machines rated for a quarter acre at half the price.

## EPOS, and why the boundary is the interesting part

Husqvarna's positioning system talks to satellites and to the national cellular
network for centimetre accuracy, and the boundary can be a physical wire or a
virtual one. That choice is worth more than it sounds.

Every other wire-free mower in this catalogue is virtual-only. If satellite
coverage in your garden turns out to be poor — a wall on one side, a mature
canopy on the other — those machines have no fallback and you are returning
them. This one can fall back to wire, which is ugly, laborious and works.

It also carries onboard radar for obstacles rather than a camera, along with
lift and tilt sensors.

## The numbers Husqvarna publishes that nobody else does

A 9.4-inch cutting width on three pivoting razor blades. Cutting height from
one to four inches, electrically adjusted. Eighty-four minutes of typical
mowing on a 5 Ah battery and 108 minutes to charge. Sixty-two decibels.

And a four-year warranty.

That last one is the number to hold against the price. One year is the norm in
this category — the Litter-Robot, the Casa Leo and most of these mowers ship
with it — and four years on an outdoor machine with a drive train is a
different kind of commitment from a brand that has been building these since
the nineties.

## What we could not find out

Husqvarna publishes more than any other maker in this category and there are
still gaps. The IP rating is not on the product page, nor is the machine's
weight, nor the number of separate zones it will manage on its own.

Eighty-four minutes of mowing against 108 minutes of charging is also a ratio
worth noticing and we cannot tell you what it means for a real lawn: it depends
entirely on how much of the day the machine is allowed to work. Husqvarna does
not publish a cuts-per-week figure and we are not going to calculate one and
present it as theirs.

## Who should buy one

Somebody with an awkwardly shaped half acre who wants the maker that has been
doing this longest, a four-year warranty, and the option of falling back to a
physical wire if the satellites disappoint. That is a real and specific
argument and nothing else here makes it.

Somebody with a steep bank should buy the LUBA 3 AWD or the Dreame A3 AWD, both
of which climb eighty per cent. Somebody with a simple open half acre and a
budget should look hard at the WORX at forty per cent of the price.
