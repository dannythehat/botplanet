**The short answer.** The Scuba V3 is the first robot in our catalogue that looks at
your pool. A single front-facing camera watches the floor ahead, recognises what is
lying on it, and steers at the dirt instead of mowing the whole pool in stripes. It
is cordless, parks itself at the waterline, charges on a contact dock, and carries
the finest filter rating of anything we list.

It is also a machine whose headline numbers are the manufacturer's own benchmarks,
whose "7 days on one charge" means something different from what it sounds like, and
which puts a camera in your pool — a sentence that deserves its own section, and
gets one.

---

## Who this is for

An in-ground pool that collects real debris — leaves, sand, twigs — where the owner
wants the robot to decide for itself what needs doing. The camera is not a gimmick
aimed at that pool: a robot that can see a drift of sand goes where the sand is,
and a blind one covers the same ground on a schedule whether it is dirty or not.

It also suits someone who wants the lightest possible routine. At 18.1 lb it is the
lightest full cleaner in our catalogue, the dock means no cable to connect, and the
app pings your phone when the robot is waiting at the waterline to be lifted out.

---

## Who should look elsewhere

- **Your pool is above ground.** Aiper lists this machine for in-ground pools.
- **You want a stated maximum pool length.** Aiper publishes an area, not a length.
  If you want a manufacturer to tell you "rated to 50 ft", this manufacturer does
  not do that — the Aiper Scuba X1 Pro Max and the Polaris FREEDOM both carry real
  length ratings.
- **You are not comfortable with a camera in the water.** Reasonable. Read the
  privacy section, then decide.
- **You keep machines for a decade.** The battery is a wear part, the same argument
  we made about the Polaris FREEDOM, at a lower price.

## What the camera actually does

Aiper's claim, from its own page: a 2-metre detection range — 6.6 ft — and more than
twenty kinds of debris recognised, from leaves down to fine sand. The robot uses that
to plan a route toward what it sees rather than sweeping blind, and Aiper says this
cuts unnecessary movement and raises cleaning efficiency "up to 10×".

Take those as what they are. The detection range and the debris list are design
specifications, and we report them as Aiper's. The 10× figure is a marketing
benchmark against an unnamed baseline — presumably Aiper's own non-camera robots —
and no independent body has measured it. A camera robot being *faster to a visible
mess* is mechanically plausible; a specific multiple is advertising.

What the camera genuinely changes is night work. The V3 carries headlights, and
Aiper markets a night-cleaning mode — a robot that navigates by sight needs to bring
its own light, and does.

---

## What "7 days on one charge" actually means

This is the claim most likely to be misread on this page, so we will spell it out.

It does not mean 168 hours of runtime. It means the robot's AI Navium mode builds a
weekly cleaning plan — sized to your pool, adjusted for weather and cleaning history
— and spreads short autonomous cleans across seven days so that one charge covers
the week. Aiper's own wording is "7 days on just one charge", and the plan is the
mechanism.

Owner research before this review recorded roughly 180 minutes of continuous
runtime, 210 in eco mode. We have not been able to re-read those figures from
Aiper's page, so treat them as researched rather than verified. Either way, the
honest framing is: about three hours of actual cleaning, budgeted intelligently
across a week.

## What it cleans, and with what

Floor, walls and the waterline, with 4,800 GPH of suction through dual brushes —
Aiper's figures, from its own page. Waterline coverage on a cordless machine at this
price is genuinely uncommon: the corded Dolphin Nautilus CC Plus does not touch the
waterline at all, and Maytronics says so.

At the end of a cycle the robot parks at the waterline and the app tells you it is
ready. You lift 18.1 lb plus water from the edge, not from the floor of the deep
end. That, more than any headline feature, is the difference between a robot that
gets used all season and one that stays in the shed.

---

## Filtration, and the finest number in our catalogue

A multi-layer basket: a 180-micron mesh for leaves and grit, backed by a 3-micron
layer that Aiper says traps sand, pollen and "invisible contaminants".

Three microns is by far the finest filtration figure of anything we list — the WYBOT
C1 publishes 180, the Betta skimmer 200, and Maytronics publishes nothing at all for
the two Dolphins we cover. Credit where due: Aiper puts a number on the thing most
manufacturers leave vague.

Keep the number in context. It is the manufacturer's rating for one layer of the
stack, not an independent lab result, and a filter that fine loads up fast in a
dirty pool. Aiper's own answer is that the coarse layer protects the fine one; the
practical answer is that the basket gets rinsed after every cycle either way.

## The camera and your privacy

A robot with a camera raises a question a robot without one never does: where do the
pictures of your garden go?

Aiper's answer, printed on its own product page, is TÜV-certified data privacy
protection. That is a real third-party certification scheme and a stronger answer
than silence — and it is also the entirety of what is published. What is processed
on the robot, what reaches Aiper's servers, and what the app can access are not
spelled out anywhere we could read.

Our position: the certification is worth something, the vagueness is worth noting,
and if a camera in the water is a line you do not want to cross, every other robot
in our catalogue cleans without one.

## Weight, the dock, and daily handling

18.1 lb — 8.2 kg — with a 955-gram battery inside, and 17.5 × 15 × 8.6 inches. All
three figures from the listing's details table, and the weight matches Aiper's own
"Featherlight" page copy exactly, which is not something we get to write often.

Charging is a wireless contact dock: set the robot on it, no cable to plug, and the
dock lives outdoors. Between the dock, the waterline parking and the app alert, the
daily routine is genuinely one-handed — which is the point of the weight.

## The name trap

Aiper's own website titles this machine the **Scuba V3 Cognitive AI** robotic pool
cleaner. The Amazon listing sells it as the **Scuba V3 AI Vision**. Same machine —
"AI Vision" is the tier descriptor on the listing, and the model, per Amazon's own
details table, is simply "Aiper Scuba V3".

Aiper publishes no SKU or model number for any Scuba machine, so the models separate
only by name and page. Two ASINs exist for the V3 and they are colour variants —
Gray and Blue — not different specifications. The machines to avoid confusing it
with are the Scuba S1, X1 and X1 Pro Max, which are different robots at different
prices, and Aiper's naming does nothing to help you.

Everything on this page describes the Gray V3, ASIN B0GG97427D.

## Not recalled, and what that does and does not mean

Two Aiper machines have been recalled: the Elite Pro, model GS100, in August 2023,
and the Seagull Pro, model ZT6001, in March 2025. The CPSC recall database holds
no others, and the Scuba V3 AI Vision is not among them.

Both faults were charging faults. One was a battery that could overheat when the
cord was plugged in without its adapter; the other was the adapter itself. Neither
describes a robot failing in the pool, which is the version of the story that
travels.

The limit of what we can say: no recall is checkable and checked. Whether owners
have filed incident reports against this model is held on SaferProducts.gov and is
not available to us as a per-model count, so we are not claiming a clean sheet —
only that the recall database is clean.

Given this is the model whose entire premium is its electronics, and given Aiper
publishes no warranty term for it at all, the storage advice matters more here
than on the cheap machines: half charge, indoors, topped up every couple of
months, never charging in the sun.

## What we cannot tell you

Written from published sources and no pool:

- **A maximum pool length.** Aiper states an area — owner research holds roughly
  1,614 sq ft — and no length at all.
- **Runtime and charge time, verified.** The 180-minute and 5-hour figures are
  research, not something we have re-read from Aiper's page.
- **A warranty term.** Aiper's site has a warranty process; we could not find a
  stated term for this machine.
- **What the 10× is measured against.** Not published.
- **What the camera records and retains.** TÜV certification is claimed; the
  details are not published.
- **How well debris recognition works on YOUR debris.** Twenty types is Aiper's
  count. Nobody here has watched it choose.

## The verdict

The Scuba V3 is the most interesting machine in our catalogue: the only one that
senses what it is cleaning, the lightest full cleaner we list, and the holder of the
finest published filter rating — at the same price as a corded Dolphin that does not
touch the waterline.

Buy it if your pool actually generates debris and you want the robot deciding where
to work. The camera earns its keep on leaves and sand, the handling is the best
here, and Aiper publishes more numbers than most of its rivals.

Hold back if you need a stated pool-length rating, a stated warranty, or certainty
about what a camera in your water does with what it sees — because on all three,
Aiper's published answer is thinner than its engineering deserves.
